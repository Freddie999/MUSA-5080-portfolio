Local Moran’s I: one house at a time

\[I_i = \frac{z_i}{m_2} \sum_j w_{ij} z_j \qquad m_2 = \frac{\sum_i z_i^2}{n} = 4{,}584\]

The average of the five local values is 0.72: the global I (with these weights).

Why not 0.60 like last week?

Last week we used plain 0/1 neighbor weights. spdep with style = "W" averages the neighbors, which gives 0.72. Same story, different weighting.

Four quadrants, read as errors

Residual = actual − predicted. High = we guessed too low.

Local Moran’s I in R

w_knn <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(st_coordinates(sales), k = 5)), style = "W")

lisa_quads <- function(resid, w, alpha = 0.01) {
  lm_perm <- localmoran_perm(resid, w, nsim = 499)
  z   <- resid - mean(resid)
  lag <- lag.listw(w, z)
  p   <- lm_perm[, "Pr(z != E(Ii)) Sim"]
  case_when(
    p >= alpha        ~ "Not significant",
    z > 0 & lag > 0   ~ "High-High",
    z < 0 & lag < 0   ~ "Low-Low",
    z > 0 & lag < 0   ~ "High-Low",
    TRUE              ~ "Low-High"
  )
}

sales$quad_1 <- lisa_quads(sales$resid_1, w_knn)

  2. What’s there that the model doesn’t know about?
  3. Can you measure it with open data?

Global vs. local

Global Moran’s I Local Moran’s I (LISA)
Output one number one number per house
Question do errors cluster? where do they cluster?
Use model log: did it fall? to-do list: what’s missing there?
Danger p-value always tiny with big n thousands of tests; chance clusters

OPERATIONALIZING (word of the week) “near”

Three ways

How Good when Watch out
Buffer count incidents within 660 ft exposure: “how much is around me?” zeros everywhere; radius is a choice
Nearest distance ft to the single closest one thing matters: a station, a park one odd point decides everything
kNN mean distance mean ft to the 5 closest smooth intensity; never zero k is a choice; far-away points count

They’re all features of X: nothing here uses a price. You’ve built the first and third already; nearest is new.

Logging a distance (rates of change): 100 → 500 ft matters, 5,000 → 5,400 ft doesn’t.

Build all three

A feature aimed at a cluster

Suppose the blue (Low–Low) pockets look like blocks with a lot of vacancy. Measure it:

tract_vac <- get_acs(geography = "tract",
                     variables = c(total = "B25002_001", vacant = "B25002_003"),
                     state = "PA", county = "Philadelphia", year = 2023,
                     geometry = TRUE, output = "wide", progress = FALSE) %>%

sales <- sales %>%
  st_join(tract_vac, join = st_nearest_feature) %>%
  mutate(pct_vacant = replace_na(pct_vacant, median(pct_vacant, na.rm = TRUE)))

sales$quad_2  <- lisa_quads(sales$resid_2, w_knn)

How to use these stats

  1. Fit. Get out-of-fold residuals.
  2. Global I → gives you a quick general (global) indication about residual clustering
  3. LISA map → shows spots that could potentially use patching up.
  4. Ask what’s there. Measure it (buffer, nearest, or kNN).
  5. Refit. Log CV RMSE and Moran’s I. Did that cluster shrink?
  6. Repeat until the clusters you can explain are gone or when you’ve spend enough time on it.

Film: How RealPage influences rent prices

CNBC, 2024 · about 10 minutes

What the software does

Inputs Model Output Decision
Public listings and market data →
The landlord’s own leases → predict rent → a recommended rent for every unit, every day → leasing manager accepts or overrides (auto-accept available)
Competing landlords’ nonpublic leases, rents, occupancy →

The modeling is A5 with rent instead of price

The fight isn’t about the regression. It’s about the third input (data competitors would never share with each other directly) and the last arrow (how hard it is to say no).

The debate:

Critics: DOJ, tenants, Philadelphia Defenders: RealPage, landlords
Data Pooling rivals’ private rents lets competitors align prices without ever talking About two-thirds of inputs are public; private data is aggregated and anonymized
Prices Recommendations push rents up, and hold them up when demand falls Rents rose because of the supply shortage, inflation, and interest rates; the software cuts rents when demand drops
Humans Alleged auto-accept and “pricing advisors” who pressed managers to comply; ex-employees told ProPublica ~90% of recommendations were adopted Managers set a different price more than half the time
Reach A law professor estimates 30–60% of multifamily units in 40+ markets The company says 7% of U.S. rentals
Efficiency Overpriced units sit empty; better pricing fills them

Sources: DOJ complaint as reported by Axios and Groundwork Collaborative (2024); ProPublica (2022).

Where it stands

Federal. DOJ sued in August 2024. The Nov 2025 settlement, now approved by the court:

  • No competitors’ nonpublic data when setting prices
  • Training on nonpublic data only if it’s at least 12 months old
  • No geographic effects finer than the state
  • Remove features that limited price cuts or aligned competitors’ prices; a court-appointed monitor
  • No admission of wrongdoing; RealPage keeps selling the product

Philadelphia. Council banned rent-coordination software unanimously in Oct 2024 (in effect Feb 2025). The first tenant suits under it were filed in July 2026 against Greystar, Bozzuto, Willow Bridge, RealPage, and Yardi. The allegations are unproven.

You’re a consulting firm now

Every table is a firm. You’ve shown you can predict prices from what a home is and where it is.

A consortium of nine corporate landlords (about 28,000 Philadelphia apartments) has issued an RFP for a rent prediction and pricing model. The budget is up to $1.8M to build plus $40k/month.

The RFP and a bid form are on your table. 12 minutes to decide:

  • Accept, modify, or decline each of eight requirements
  • Your price, including any premium for requirements you think are risky
  • Conditions you attach
  • Your line (what you won’t do for any price) and your premium (what you’d do for more)

The RFP at a glance

R1 Market rent for every unit, from public data
R2 Train on members’ pooled, current, nonpublic rent rolls
R3 Daily recommended asking and renewal rents
R4 Recommendations accepted by default; overriding needs a manager’s sign-off
R5 Block-level geography
R6 Optimize revenue, not occupancy: vacancy at the right price beats leasing below market
R7 Quarterly report comparing each member’s rents to the consortium average by neighborhood
R8 Confidential: nothing disclosed to tenants, journalists, or regulators beyond the law

Scored on price (40%), revenue lift (40%), technical approach (20%). Declining costs you points.

Rounds

  1. Pitches: 60 seconds per firm. (8 min)
  2. The award: the consortium (me) picks the winner on its own scoring. Then the class votes on a second question: which bid would you defend on the front page of the Inquirer? (3 min)
  3. The reveal: what Philadelphia’s ordinance and the federal settlement say about each requirement. (5 min)
